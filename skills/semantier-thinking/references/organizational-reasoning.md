# Organizational reasoning — roles, incentives and systemic mechanisms

**Load only when:** formal organizational decisions, collective action, hierarchy, incentives, institutions or recurring feedback patterns have a material effect on the question. Do not apply systems jargon to a purely individual event.

1. Define the organizational outcome, actors and time horizon. Separate **officially stated policy** from observed incentives, practices and behaviors; do not infer motives as fact.
2. Distinguish direct observed incident from repeated pattern. Analyze who controls information, who can act, who bears consequences, and how institutional constraints differ from personal preference.
3. When patterns warrant it, hypothesize specific structures: resource bottleneck, principal–agent conflict, incentive misalignment, power asymmetry, bureaucracy, group definitions or reinforcing/balancing feedback.
4. Test competing mechanisms against evidence: timing, organizational boundary, stocks/flows and delays. Feedback-loop arrows must represent defensible causal relations; a diagram is not proof.
5. Identify an intervention with clear owner, measurable effect, downside, monitoring horizon and plausible fallback; distinguish local optimization from whole-system effects.
6. If stakeholder rights/duties materially conflict, load [ethical reasoning](ethical-reasoning.md) only for that normative issue; otherwise do not stack methods.

**L1:** distinguish description from one inferred organizational mechanism. **L2:** review roles, incentives and alternatives. **L3:** source-grounded mechanism/feedback analysis, competing hypotheses, second-order effects and testable interventions.

**Book basis:** Paul–Elder chapter 13 treats covert power struggle, group definitions of reality, bureaucracy and apparent success as obstacles to reasonable organizational thinking; related group effects appear in chapter 11.

**Avoid:** diagnosing personnel character, asserting the hidden cause, “everything is a complex system,” invented loops, or treating compliance with a formal rule as proof of good outcome.
