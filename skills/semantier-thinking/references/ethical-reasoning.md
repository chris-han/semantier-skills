# Ethical reasoning — normative inquiry, not a single outcome maximizer

**Load only when:** rights, duties, fairness, consent, harm or legitimate values materially influence the current inquiry, decision or communication. A mention of an ethics-related topic is not sufficient.

1. Identify who is affected and by what proposed action, including nonparticipants. Separate verified facts, empirical uncertainty, conflicting preferences and normative premises.
2. Identify applicable **legal duties** only from current jurisdictional legal evidence; distinguish what is legally allowed/required from what is morally justified, culturally customary or religiously prescribed.
3. Select relevant ethical lenses rather than scoring every framework mechanically:
   - Duties and rights (what must not be done regardless of aggregate benefit).
   - Consequences and proportionality (who gains, who bears burdens, reversible or irreversible harm).
   - Fairness/justice (consistency, unequal power, distribution and procedural legitimacy).
   - Care/relationships and vulnerability (concrete duties to those affected).
   - Character/integrity where a decision centers on professional trust.
4. State the strongest reasonable objection and distinguish actual value disagreement from disagreement over facts. Do not invent a unanimous moral answer or impose numerical weights without user authority.
5. Report which conclusions survive across lenses, which depend on values or contested evidence, what options preserve rights and what requires human decision.

**L1:** isolate one normative distinction and respond. **L2:** compare stakeholder claims and relevant lenses. **L3:** full rights/duties/consequence/procedural audit, dated legal-source qualification and material residual conflict. A legal opinion belongs to qualified jurisdiction-specific review.

**Book basis:** Paul–Elder, chapter 12, distinguishing ethics from law, religion, social conventions and taboos. **Do not convert these into equivalences.**
